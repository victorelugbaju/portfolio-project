Concept project

I designed a food delivery mobile app focused on quick browsing, fast checkout, and clear order tracking. It is aimed at people who want to decide, order, and receive food without delay or confusion. The project covers onboarding, discovery, ordering, and post-purchase states, with an emphasis on reducing steps and keeping the interface focused at every stage.

03 — Problem & solution What was wrong, what I did
The problem

Someone opening a food delivery app to order dinner often hesitates, not because of lack of options, but because the process feels slow and unclear. Too many categories, crowded screens, and long checkout flows interrupt momentum. After ordering, they are left uncertain about what is happening next. The issue is not access to food, but the time and mental effort required to move from browsing to receiving it.

The solution

I designed a focused flow that moves from discovery to checkout in a small number of clear steps, then shifts attention to tracking after purchase. The interface reduces visible options at each stage and highlights only the next action. I prioritised speed and clarity over depth. The trade-off is that advanced filtering and detailed customisation are limited, which reduces flexibility for users who want more control.

04 — Design process Four steps
01

Audit

I reviewed common food delivery flows and broke them into stages: browse, select, pay, track. I noted where screens became crowded or required extra decisions. I then reduced the flow to the fewest necessary steps without removing essential information.

02

Structure

I mapped the navigation to five primary areas and removed anything that did not need constant access. I defined what belongs in flows versus global navigation, ensuring checkout and cart stayed contextual instead of persistent tabs.

03

Screens

I designed key screens starting from home to order success, focusing on hierarchy and spacing. I created card-based layouts with clear actions and limited text, making sure each screen had a single dominant action.

04

Refine

I aligned onboarding visuals with actual product screens instead of illustrations. I adjusted spacing, typography, and states to keep consistency across onboarding, ordering, and tracking, ensuring the experience felt like one continuous system.

06 — What I learned

I initially tried to show too much on each screen, thinking more options meant better usability. It made the flow slower and harder to scan. This project pushed me to remove features and focus on sequence instead of density. I learned to design around what happens next, not everything that could happen.

  • I kept cart and checkout contextual rather than persistent tabs, which freed the navigation to hold only five areas.
  • Limiting filtering and customisation made the flow faster, but it removed control from anyone who shops with specific constraints.
  • I would now design onboarding from real product screens first, instead of building illustrations I later had to replace.
