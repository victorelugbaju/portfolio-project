Designing grocery shopping. For busy homes.

We designed Groovia as a self-directed concept for grocery shopping, focused on busy parents managing work and home routines. The app centres on quick selection, saved lists, and scheduled delivery rather than open-ended browsing. We worked through flows from discovery to checkout, defining how people move from a planned list to a confirmed order without losing time or context.

03 — Problem & solution What was wrong, what I did
The problem

Grocery shopping often breaks down at the point of repetition. A parent knows what they need, but still has to search, scroll, and rebuild the same cart each week. Delays, missing items, and unclear delivery timing add more friction. The problem shows up when someone tries to quickly restock essentials but gets pulled into a slow, cluttered process that doesn't reflect how predictable their needs already are.

The solution

We designed Groovia around list-first shopping. Users build or reuse a shopping list, then move it directly into a cart and schedule delivery in a few steps. Tracking focuses on the full order rather than individual items. This approach reduces unnecessary browsing, but it also limits discovery, promotions, and impulse buying. We chose to prioritise speed and predictability, knowing it makes the experience less flexible for casual shoppers.

04 — Design process Four steps
01

Research

We spoke with people who regularly handle household shopping and mapped how they plan, buy, and restock groceries. We focused on repeated behaviour, not one-off purchases, and noted where time was lost or decisions felt unnecessary.

02

Structure

We mapped the core flow from list creation to checkout, keeping it linear and short. We removed optional paths like deep browsing and focused on clear transitions between list, cart, and delivery scheduling.

03

Wireframes

We sketched low-fidelity screens for onboarding, shop, cart, and tracking. We tested how many steps each action required and simplified where possible, especially around adding items and confirming orders.

04

UI

We designed high-fidelity screens with a clean layout and clear hierarchy. We defined product cards, filters, and tracking states, making sure each screen supported quick decisions without adding visual noise.

06 — What I learned

We initially leaned too much into features like promotions and discovery before focusing on the actual problem. This project pushed us to prioritise repeated behaviour over variety. We learned to design around what people already know they need, instead of trying to influence what they might want, and to accept the trade-offs that come with that decision.

  • We reduced the flow to move from list to checkout in a few steps, removing extra browsing layers.
  • We chose to track full orders instead of items, which simplified the interface but removed detailed visibility.
  • I would now test the list-first approach earlier, instead of refining UI before validating that core behaviour.
