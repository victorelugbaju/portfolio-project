Seamfix is an enterprise asset-tracking system for organisations that need to manage company-owned assets throughout their lifecycle. I designed workflows for Admins, Asset Managers, Employees and Auditors, covering authentication, governance, operational tasks, reporting and audit visibility while keeping role-based access and long-term scalability at the centre of every design decision.

03 — Problem & solution What was wrong, what I did
The problem

An Asset Manager could register, assign and recover hundreds of assets, but every action depended on accurate records, clear ownership and reliable history. When information was scattered or difficult to verify, routine tasks became slower and mistakes became harder to trace. Administrators needed organisation-wide oversight, employees needed straightforward self-service, and auditors needed confidence that every important change could be reviewed without relying on manual records or memory.

The solution

I designed the platform around the complete asset lifecycle instead of isolated features. Each role received a focused dashboard and workflows tailored to its responsibilities, while shared patterns such as notifications, audit logs, search and permissions remained consistent across the product. The trade-off was accepting a larger upfront investment in reusable systems and role mapping before moving into detailed screen design, which slowed early progress but created a more consistent foundation.

04 — Design process Four steps
01

Journey mapping

I mapped the complete lifecycle from account provisioning to audit reporting, then broke each journey into role-specific tasks. This exposed where responsibilities overlapped and where each role needed different information, permissions and navigation.

02

Information architecture

I organised the product into reusable modules instead of separate feature groups. Authentication, asset management, reporting, notifications and governance became consistent structures that could support every user role without duplicating patterns.

03

Workflow design

I designed detailed flows for authentication, dashboard access and user management before expanding into operational workflows. I considered loading, empty, error and permission states alongside the primary journeys instead of treating them as later additions.

04

System refinement

I standardised interface patterns including tables, status badges, forms, filters, modals and notification behaviour. This helped keep interactions consistent as the product grew across multiple modules and user types.

06 — What I learned

I realised I had previously focused too much on individual screens before fully understanding how responsibilities changed across different user roles. On this project I worked from workflows first, then designed interfaces around those decisions. That approach made it easier to keep navigation, permissions and shared components consistent across a much larger enterprise product.

  • Designing role permissions early prevented later conflicts between operational workflows and governance requirements.
  • Planning loading, empty and error states alongside primary flows produced more complete designs than adding them afterwards.
  • I would spend more time validating the information architecture before designing high-fidelity screens, especially for large enterprise platforms.
